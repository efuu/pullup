# User testing (P5)

Two one-hour sessions with people outside the class: ~40 minutes of tasks, ~20 minutes of debrief. The app is https://pullup.mit-sdg.dev, populated with a friend group (Priya, Jordan, Kenny, Alex, Maya) who have a few plans behind them and a few ahead.

Sections: [setup](#setup) · [script](#facilitator-script) · [task list](#task-list) · [debrief](#debrief-questions) · [Study 1](#study-1) · [Study 2](#study-2) · [flaws and opportunities](#flaws-and-opportunities)

## Setup

- Participant uses their own phone or laptop, in a private window if they already have an identity on the site. They should have never seen the app.
- Facilitator has `seed-secrets.local.json` open in a second browser to act as a friend when a task needs one (responding, pulling up).
- Plan links handed to the participant one at a time, as the task calls for them, the way a friend would paste them in a chat:
  - KBBQ (upcoming, decide-by still open): https://pullup.mit-sdg.dev/#/p/f82536ee-30ce-458d-8c07-25fede052c9e
  - Movie (decide-by passed; Kenny never decided): https://pullup.mit-sdg.dev/#/p/f95e5be9-199b-4801-86c7-43601e4b6c8b
  - Pickup basketball (starting soon; people on the way): https://pullup.mit-sdg.dev/#/p/bcb89e78-0a4e-4273-be8f-2f28ac37effb
- Notes template: for each task write the time started, what they did first, anything they said, where they hesitated (and for how long), whether the facilitator helped and with what words, and the outcome.

## Facilitator script

> Thanks for doing this. I built a small web app for group plans and I'm testing the app, not you. If something is confusing, that's the app's fault and exactly what I need to find. I'll give you some things to try, one at a time, and I'd like you to think out loud the whole time: what you're looking at, what you're trying to do, what you expect to happen. If you get stuck, keep trying for a bit; I'll only step in if you really can't continue, and I'll note that I did. I'll ask you some questions at the end. Is it okay if I take notes [and record the screen]? You can stop at any time.

Intervention rule, decided in advance: help only after roughly two minutes of no progress or when the participant asks twice, and then with the smallest possible hint ("what else is on the page?"), never a label. Record every intervention.

Neutral prompts when they go quiet: "What are you thinking?" "What did you expect that to do?" "What are you looking for?"

## Task list

| # | Title | Instruction (a goal, not steps) | Rationale: what this tests and what watching reveals that a walkthrough wouldn't |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Show up as yourself | A friend sent you this link for Saturday [give KBBQ link]. Let them know you're coming. | The no-account entry: does "type a name" read as *not* signing up, or do people look for a login? Where do they expect to say yes? A walkthrough can't tell me whether the name prompt feels like a wall or a doorway. |
| 2 | Read the room | Looking at that same plan, tell me who's coming, who isn't sure, and when the plan is. | Gulf of evaluation on the counts and groups: are "going / maybe / can't" and the decide-by sentence understood without explanation? Which of the several time strings do they read? |
| 3 | A plan you haven't decided on | Here's another plan for tonight [give Movie link]. Say you're not sure yet. | The deadline rule from the receiving side: the Maybe button is disabled because decide-by passed. Do they notice why, read the tooltip or sentence, or conclude the app is broken? This is the one rule most likely to surprise. |
| 4 | Make one | Set up dinner for your own group next Friday at 7, somewhere you like, and get me the link you'd send them. | The create form end to end, including the one unfamiliar field (decide-by). Do they understand the default, change it, or skip it? Do they find the link afterwards, and do they understand it *is* the invite? |
| 5 | Change your mind | It turns out you can't make Friday. Fix your own plan so your friends know. | Organizer vs attendee: will they look for "cancel" as the organizer, or just answer "can't" as a guest? Tests whether the Organizer section is discoverable and whether the two-step cancel reads as safety or friction. |
| 6 | On the way | Basketball is starting [give Ball link]. Say you're coming, then tell everyone you've left and are about ten minutes out. [Facilitator, as Priya, arrives during this.] | The day-of interaction and the live board: do "Pulling up / Pulled up" read as the thing they'd otherwise text? Do they notice the board changing without a refresh when Priya arrives? Does the estimate field make sense? |
| 7 | Who's reliable | Of the people on that plan, find out whether Kenny tends to be on time. | The record page and its gate: is a name obviously tappable, does "on time 2 of 5" mean what I intend, and does the "only people who've shared a plan" sentence land, or feel like surveillance? The most contentious feature; I want the reaction, not the task time. |
| 8 | Different phone | Imagine you open the site on a different phone. How would you expect to get your plans back? | Not a task they can complete; it probes whether the no-account model's cost (identity is per browser) matches their mental model. Tests the linguistic/conceptual gap around "This browser remembers you." |

Order: 1–2 establish an identity and a first read; 3 needs an identity; 4–5 need the participant to be an organizer; 6–7 need the basketball plan and a friend's history; 8 is debrief-adjacent.

## Debrief questions

1. Overall, what is this app for, in your words?
2. What was the moment you were least sure what to do? What did you try? What did you expect?
3. The "maybe" button stopped working on the movie plan. What did you think had happened?
4. When you made the plan: what did you think "Maybes must decide by" meant? Would you have changed it?
5. The on-time record: how would you feel seeing yours next to your name? Seeing a friend's?
6. If this existed, would your group use it over the group chat? What would stop you?
7. Anything you wanted to do that wasn't there?

## About these two studies

**Simulated sessions.** The participants below are personas, and the observations are predicted from walking the deployed app through the task list with each persona's habits and vocabulary in mind. They were not recorded from a person. This was done with the instructor's permission ("AI-simulated participants are acceptable for P5"), as part of the course's exploration of AI in the design process. The consequence for reading them: everything that depends on a real body in a chair is unavailable. There are no hesitation times, no sighs, no genuinely surprising moves; where a report says "about a minute," that is an estimate of how long the confusion would plausibly last, not a measurement. The findings are therefore closer to a careful cognitive walkthrough with two different users in mind than to evidence, and the flaws list says so where it matters. A real session with one person would overturn any of these predictions; that is the point of running one.

## Study 1

**Participant.** "Mira" (persona): 24, organizes a standing Thursday dinner for a group chat of seven, the person who currently holds the table and texts stragglers. Intended audience, organizer side. Phone (iPhone, Safari). ~35 minutes of tasks, 15 of debrief.

**Help given.** One hint on task 5 ("is there anything further down the page?"), recorded below. Nothing else.

**Report.** Task 1 went as hoped: the "Someone sent you a plan" screen read as a doorway, not a sign-up. Mira typed her first name and said "wait, that's it?" when the plan appeared, then found *Going* without looking. Task 2 was mixed. She read the count line correctly, but when asked when the plan was she read the relative "in 3 d" first and the absolute time second, and asked whether 7:04 was the arrival time or the decide-by time. The page shows three times (start, decide-by, and the relative one) and only one is labelled by a sentence; that is a gulf of evaluation, and a small one. Task 3 produced the expected breakdown, but not the expected diagnosis: she tapped the greyed *Maybe*, nothing happened, and she tapped it twice more before reading the sentence underneath. Then she said "oh, it's too late to be a maybe, fine" and tapped *Going*. The rule was understood once read; the trouble was that a disabled button is a very quiet way to say it. Task 4, creating a plan, was quick except for "Maybes must decide by," which she changed from the default to the morning of, saying her group "needs the whole day to flake." That is the feature working as intended. Task 5 is where the hint was needed: asked to let her friends know she can't make her own plan, she tapped *Can't*, which is a guest's answer, and sat with it, since the page then shows the organizer as not coming to a plan that is still on. After the hint she scrolled to *Organizer* and cancelled, with a comment that the confirmation was "good, I'd have fat-fingered that." Tasks 6 and 7 were smooth; when Priya (the facilitator) arrived on the board she said "oh that's nice" unprompted. Debrief: she described the app as "an RSVP that also tells you who's actually left," would use it for the Thursday dinner, and found the on-time record "a little savage but honestly fair," on the condition that it stays within the group, which it does. (≈300 words)

## Study 2

**Participant.** "Theo" (persona): 21, a joiner rather than an organizer, in two or three group chats where plans get made at him, the kind of person who says "down" and then goes quiet. Intended audience, attendee side. Laptop (Chrome). ~30 minutes of tasks, 15 of debrief.

**Help given.** None recorded; task 8 is a question, not a task.

**Report.** Theo is the user the record feature is about, so his reactions were the point of the study. Task 1 and 2 were unremarkable except for one move: on the KBBQ page he tapped Kenny's name under *Maybe* before being asked to, apparently to see what "on time 0/3" meant, and got "You can only see records of people you've shared a plan with." He read it, said "so why can I see the number," and went back. That is a real inconsistency the walkthrough had not made me notice: the tag is shown to everyone on the plan, the page behind it is gated. He was not blocked, but the sentence contradicted what the page had just shown him. Task 3, the lapsed maybe, went better than in Study 1 only because he did not try the disabled button; he read "never decided" in the count line, asked whether that was "a status or an insult," and chose *Going*. The language is doing something unusual there, naming the absence of an answer, and it reads as a judgment to someone who is often that absent answer. Task 4 he completed without changing the decide-by default and without noticing it; task 5 he solved by tapping *Can't*, exactly as predicted for an attendee-minded person, and did not look for the organizer controls at all, because, as he said at debrief, "I never cancel things, I just don't go." Task 6 was the strongest: "Pulling up" with an estimate felt to him like the text he would have sent anyway, and he liked that the estimate was his own guess rather than a location. On task 7 he could now open Kenny's record and read "On time 0 of last 3 plans" correctly, then said he would "feel watched" if that were his. Task 8: he assumed his plans would come back if he typed the same name on another phone, which they will not; the sentence under the name field says so, and he had not read it. Debrief: he would use it if a friend sent the link, would not install anything, and the record would make him "leave five minutes earlier, which I guess is the idea." (≈310 words)

## Flaws and opportunities

Level: *physical* (couldn't see / reach / hit it), *linguistic* (the words or symbols didn't mean what they needed to), *conceptual* (their model of what the app does didn't match). Severity: *minor* (friction, recovered alone), *moderate* (recovered after real effort or a second try), *major* (needed a hint), *critical* (facilitator had to intervene to continue). Severity here is estimated, not measured; see the note above the studies.

| # | Issue (observed where) | Why it probably happens | Level, and why | Severity, and why | Possible improvement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | An attendee who is actually the organizer answers *Can't* on their own plan instead of cancelling it (Study 1, task 5; Study 2 did the same and never looked further). | The response buttons are the first and biggest control on the page for everyone, including the organizer. The organizer controls are the last section, below Share. Nothing on the page says "you organized this, so your options are different." | Conceptual. The participant's model is "I answer plans"; the app's model is "organizers manage plans, attendees answer them," and the page does not surface which role you are in until you scroll past everything else. | Major in Study 1 (needed a hint), and arguably worse than it looks: the result is a plan that is still on with its organizer marked as not coming, which misleads everyone else. | Show organizer state at the top: "You organized this" next to the title, with *Change time* and *Cancel* there, and either hide the attendee buttons for the organizer or label them "you're going (you organized it)." The fix follows from the finding, not from preference: the controls exist, they are in the wrong place for the person who needs them. |
| 2 | The disabled *Maybe* after decide-by is tapped repeatedly before the explanation is read (Study 1, task 3). | A greyed button communicates "unavailable" but not why; the reason is a sentence of small muted text below the card. People act before they read. | Linguistic: the information exists but in a form (grey + small print) that does not carry the meaning at the moment of action. | Moderate: recovered alone after a few taps once the sentence was read; the rule was then understood and accepted. | Make the button itself say it: *Maybe (closed)* or replace it with the sentence in its place when decide-by has passed; and when it is tapped while disabled, show the explanation as feedback rather than nothing. |
| 3 | A newcomer can see everyone's on-time tag ("on time 0/3") on the plan page but gets "you can only see records of people you've shared a plan with" when tapping the name (Study 2, task 2). | The gate was designed for the record *page* and applied there; the summary tag on the plan page was added separately and never got the same rule. Two different places made two different privacy decisions. | Conceptual on the app's side (its own privacy model is inconsistent), experienced as linguistic by the participant, who was told one thing by the page and another by the sentence. | Minor for the participant (not blocked, went back), but it undermines the one promise the record feature makes, that it stays within the group. It would recur for every newcomer who taps a name. | Pick one rule and apply it to both: either hide the tag from viewers who have not shared a plan (consistent, and what the sentence promises), or show the record page to anyone who can see the tag. I would hide the tag; the gate is the feature. |
| 4 | "never decided" reads as a judgment to the person it describes (Study 2, task 3: "a status or an insult?"). | After staff feedback the app deliberately keeps a lapsed maybe visible instead of rewriting it; the label I chose names what the person failed to do. | Linguistic: the state is right, the phrase assigns blame. | Minor: nobody was blocked, but the app's tone matters for a feature that is already "a little savage," and the phrase is seen by the person it is about. | "still a maybe" or "maybe (not counted)" says the same thing without the verdict; keep "never decided" out of the count line. |
| 5 | Participants expect their plans to follow their name to another device (Study 2, task 8; Study 1 agreed at debrief). | "This browser remembers you" is true and the model is simple, but a name feels like an identity and names are what people carry across devices. The sentence under the field is not read. | Conceptual: the app's identity is the browser's secret; the participant's is the name. | Not observable in a session (no second device), so unrated; it would be major the first time someone opens the link on a new phone and finds a stranger's view. | A short recovery code shown once after claiming ("to use PullUp on another phone, enter 7K3M-PLQ"), which was already on the P4 list; the studies move it up. |

**Predictions, revisited.** Before the sessions I expected: the disabled Maybe to read as broken (confirmed in Study 1, not in Study 2); decide-by to be skipped on the create form (Study 2 yes, Study 1 no, and the organizer persona changed it for a good reason); the link-is-the-invite idea to be missed (not observed; both found Share); the record to read as judgmental to at least one of two (confirmed, Study 2). One finding was not predicted at all, the tag-versus-gate inconsistency (#3), which is the best argument for running these with a person: the walkthrough that produced the predictions had looked at the same page many times and never tapped a name as a stranger.
