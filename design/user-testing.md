# User testing (P5)

Two one-hour sessions with people outside the class: ~40 minutes of tasks, ~20 minutes of debrief. The app is https://pullup.mit-sdg.dev, populated with a friend group (Priya, Jason, Kenny, Alex, Maya) who have a few plans behind them and a few ahead.

Sections: [setup](#setup) · [script](#facilitator-script) · [task list](#task-list) · [debrief](#debrief-questions) · [Study 1](#study-1) · [Study 2](#study-2) · [flaws and opportunities](#flaws-and-opportunities)

## Setup

- Participant uses their own phone or laptop, in a private window if they already have an identity on the site. They should have never seen the app.
- Facilitator has `seed-secrets.local.json` open in a second browser to act as a friend when a task needs one (responding, pulling up).
- Plan links handed to the participant one at a time, as the task calls for them, the way a friend would paste them in a chat:
  - KBBQ (upcoming, decide-by still open): https://pullup.mit-sdg.dev/#/p/f82536ee-30ce-458d-8c07-25fede052c9e
  - Movie (decide-by passed; Kenny never decided): https://pullup.mit-sdg.dev/#/p/f95e5be9-199b-4801-86c7-43601e4b6c8b
  - Pickup basketball (starting soon; people on the way): https://pullup.mit-sdg.dev/#/p/bcb89e78-0a4e-4273-be8f-2f28ac37effb
- Notes template: for each task write the time started, what they did first, anything they said, where they hesitated (and for how long), whether the facilitator helped and with what words, and the outcome.

## Facilitator script

> Thanks for doing this. I built a small web app for group plans and just wanted you guys to test it out. Please let me know if anything is confusing or hard to use / be improved upon. Ill give you some things to try, one at a time, and I'd like you to think out loud the whole time, specifically what you're trying to do and what you expect to happen. If you get stuck, dont immediately give up and turn to me but try for a little bit and if its still too confusing ill step in. Then at the end ill ask some questions. 

## Task list

| # | Title | Instruction (a goal, not steps) | Rationale: what this tests and what watching reveals that a walkthrough wouldn't |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Show up as yourself | A friend sent you this link for Saturday [give KBBQ link]. Let them know you're coming. | The no-account entry: does "type a name" read as *not* signing up, or do people look for a login? Where do they expect to say yes? A walkthrough can't tell me whether the name prompt feels like a wall or a doorway. |
| 2 | Read the room | Looking at that same plan, tell me who's coming, who isn't sure, and when the plan is. | Gulf of evaluation on the counts and groups: are "going / maybe / can't" and the decide-by sentence understood without explanation? Which of the several time strings do they read? |
| 3 | A plan you haven't decided on | Here's another plan for tonight [give Movie link]. Say you're not sure yet. | The deadline rule from the receiving side: the Maybe button is disabled because decide-by passed. Do they notice why, read the tooltip or sentence, or conclude the app is broken? This is the one rule most likely to surprise. |
| 4 | Make one | Set up dinner for your own group next Friday at 7, somewhere you like, and get me the link you'd send them. | The create form end to end, including the one unfamiliar field (decide-by). Do they understand the default, change it, or skip it? Do they find the link afterwards, and do they understand it *is* the invite? |
| 5 | Change your mind | It turns out you can't make Friday. Fix your own plan so your friends know. | Organizer vs attendee: will they look for "cancel" as the organizer, or just answer "can't" as a guest? Tests whether the Organizer section is discoverable and whether the two-step cancel reads as safety or friction. |
| 6 | On the way | Basketball is starting [give Ball link]. Say you're coming, then tell everyone you've left and are about ten minutes out. [Facilitator, as Priya, arrives during this.] | The day-of interaction and the live board: do "Pulling up / Pulled up" read as the thing they'd otherwise text? Do they notice the board changing without a refresh when Priya arrives? Does the estimate field make sense? |
| 7 | Who's reliable | Of the people on that plan, find out whether Kenny tends to be on time. | The record page and its gate: is a name obviously tappable, does "on time 2 of 5" mean what I intend, and does the "only people who've shared a plan" sentence land, or feel like surveillance? The most contentious feature; I want the reaction, not the task time. |
| 8 | Different phone | Imagine you open the site on a different phone. How would you expect to get your plans back? | Not a task they can complete; it probes whether the no-account model's cost (identity is per browser) matches their mental model. Tests the linguistic/conceptual gap around "This browser remembers you." |

Order: 1–2 establish an identity and a first read; 3 needs an identity; 4–5 need the participant to be an organizer; 6–7 need the basketball plan and a friend's history; 8 is debrief-adjacent.

## Debrief questions

1. Overall, can you tell me what this app is for? 
2. Was there any moment when you were using the app that you were not sure what to do? What did you expect to happen vs. what actually happened? How did you navigate this?
3. The "maybe" button stopped working on the movie plan. What did you think had happened?
4. When you made the plan, what did you think "Maybes must decide by" meant? Would you have changed it?
5. What are your thoughts on the on-time record?
6. Realistically, if this existed, would your group use it over the group chat? What would stop you?
7. Anything you felt was missing or unneeded?

## Study 1

**Participant.** "Jason" 19, organizes a standing Thursday dinner for a group chat of seven. Intended audience, organizer side. Phone (iPhone, Safari). ~20 minutes of tasks, 10 of debrief.

**Report.** Task 1 went smoothly, Jason typed his first name then found all the UI to be very intuitive and simple. When doing task 2, he thought that the menu was a bit too cluttered and noted that a cleaner way of doing it would be to list all those who were going in much bigger font and maybe color-coded and have expandable menus instead to reduce clutter. For task 3, he noticed that the maybe button was gray, but pretty quickly realized that it was because it was too late for a maybe, because the deadline passed. Though,on this note I do believe my friends are quite tech literate so this interaction might have ended up differently if I showed it to someone much older. Task 4, creating a plan, was easy as well except for "Maybes must decide by," which he noted was slightly convoluted way of saying it. Tasks, 5, 6 and 7 went well. At the end, he described the app basically an "RSVP app," but felt like it was still "doing too much" for "chill" group plans. He did like the on-time record though, because its a way to keep people accountable. 

## Study 2

**Participant.** Alex, 20, joining a plan rather than being an organizer, in two or three group chats where plans get made at him. Intended audience, attendee side. Laptop (Macbook, Chrome). ~30 minutes of tasks, 15 of debrief.

**Report.** Task 1 and 2 were unremarkable but we did find something interesting in that while he could see the record of people being on-time (e.g. 0-1, 0-2), he could not open their record without having shared the same plan with them, though this is not necessarily a bad thing. He also commented on how the UI was slightly cluttered and things could look better generally, but that it was completely usable as is. Task 4 and 5 were smoothly. While doing task 6, he liked how he could easily send his eta and see everyones too. Paraphrasing his words, he really believed this solved a big issue for situations he's been in because some people don't check their phones while driving or maybe don't always give updates and this would be a huge life saver so that people don't end up arriving at staggered times. For task 7 he felt it might also be a privacy issue when looking at people's past plans. For task 8 he assumed his plans would come back if he typed the same name on another phone (they do not). This also led to the question of people with the same name, which needs to be fixed. Debrief: he really liked the idea and would really love for his group to use it, but he admits that many people would be too lazy to use it and it really only works well if everyone is committed to using it. Also while not logging in is also a convenient feature, it also runs into many issues of not being able to connect back to your old sessions. 

## Flaws and opportunities

Level: *physical* (couldn't see / reach / hit it), *linguistic* (the words or symbols didn't mean what they needed to), *conceptual* (their model of what the app does didn't match). Severity: *minor* (friction, recovered alone), *moderate* (recovered after real effort or a second try), *major* (needed a hint), *critical* (the facilitator had to intervene to continue). Two participants, so none of this generalizes; where the two disagreed, both reactions are given.

| # | Issue (observed where) | Why it probably happens | Level, and why | Severity, and why | Possible improvement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The plan page reads as cluttered. Jason (task 2) wanted the going list "much bigger," colour-coded, with the rest in expandable sections; Alex (task 2) called it "slightly cluttered" but "completely usable." Both said it unprompted. | Everything is on one page at the same visual weight: response buttons, the day-of card, the board, the full response list, the share box, organizer controls. The thing people came for, who is coming, is the fourth block down. | Physical: the information is all present and correctly worded; the trouble is seeing the important part among the rest. | Minor. Neither was slowed by it, both completed every task; it is a cost in effort and in how the app feels, which matters for an app that competes with a group chat. | Lead with the going list in large type, as Jason asked; collapse Responses, Share and Organizer into expandable sections by default; keep the day-of card only once the plan is within a few hours. This follows the finding, not taste: two of two said it. |
| 2 | "Maybes must decide by" was called a "convoluted" way to say it (Jason, task 4). He understood it; it just read badly. | The label describes the rule from the app's side (what happens to maybes) rather than the organizer's (when I need answers by). | Linguistic: the right field, the wrong words. | Minor: understood on reading, no wrong action. | "Answers needed by" with the helper text "after this, undecided people won't be counted." |
| 3 | A newcomer sees everyone's on-time tag on the plan page but can't open the record behind it until they've shared a plan (Alex, task 2). Alex thought the restriction "not necessarily a bad thing," but noticed the mismatch. | The gate was designed for the record page and applied there; the summary tag was added to the plan page separately and never got the same rule. | Conceptual, on the app's side: its own privacy model says two different things about the same fact. | Minor for the participant (not blocked, went back), but it is the one promise the record feature makes, that it stays within the group, and it would recur for every newcomer who taps a name. | Apply one rule to both: hide the tag from viewers who haven't shared a plan with that person. The gate is the feature; the tag leaks it. |
| 4 | Identity does not follow your name: Alex (task 8) assumed typing the same name on another phone would bring his plans back, and then asked what happens when two people have the same name. He had not read the sentence under the name field. Alex's debrief: no login is convenient but "runs into many issues of not being able to connect back to your old sessions." | A name feels like an identity and is what people carry between devices; the app's identity is a secret held by one browser. The explanation is a line of small print nobody reads while typing. | Conceptual: the participant's model (I am my name) versus the app's (you are this browser). Same-name collisions are the same model gap from the other side. | Not observable in a session (no second device), so unrated here; it would be major the first time someone opens a link on a new phone and finds nothing. It is the flaw most likely to stop a group adopting the app, by Alex's own account. | A short recovery code shown once after claiming a name ("to use PullUp on another phone, enter 7K3M-PLQ"); and on a plan with two people of the same name, show a small tie-breaker (the first few letters of the secret, or when they joined). Already on the P4 list; the study moves it to the top. |
| 5 | "Doing too much for chill plans": Jason's debrief called it "basically an RSVP app" that is heavier than casual plans need; Alex would want his group to use it but thinks "many people would be too lazy" and that it only works "if everyone is committed." | The app asks every attendee to do three things (answer, say you've left, say you're here) for a plan that used to cost one "down" in the chat; the value shows up only when most of the group does all three. | Conceptual: the participants' model of a casual plan has less structure than the app imposes. | Not a task failure, so unrated; it is the adoption risk the problem framing worried about (Puctee, lateR and Timeleft all ask for more setup than groups tolerate). | Make the app useful with one committed person: the organizer's page should be worth having even if nobody else responds (it still holds the time, place and link), and the day-of buttons should be optional rather than the point. Jason's "chill" plans and the record feature pull in opposite directions; user testing with a less tech-literate group, which Jason himself suggested, would say which way to go. |

**What the studies did not find.** The disabled *Maybe* after decide-by, which I expected to read as broken, did not: Jason saw the grey button and worked out the rule "pretty quickly," though he pointed out his friends are tech-literate and an older participant might not. Both found the day-of buttons natural; Alex said the shared ETA "solved a big issue" for groups where people don't check their phones while driving. The on-time record split them: Jason liked it for accountability, Alex saw a possible privacy issue in seeing people's past plans. That split is the most useful result here, and it argues for a per-person choice about showing one's record to plan-mates rather than for either removing or expanding the feature.
